Enough food, out of reach

Problem statement and objectives

The question we set out to answer

The Datathon challenge: explore the world's food systems through data, from farm to fork.

In years when Zambia grows enough calories, why can most households still not eat a healthy diet, and could they grow it themselves?

Who this is for

PolicymakersMinistry of Agriculture teams who design input subsidies and food programmes.
Extension officers and NGOsPeople who advise farming households district by district.
Smallholder householdsThe families whose income, land and seed decide what reaches the plate.

  1. Acquire. Downloaded five FAOSTAT domains for Zambia.
  2. Clean and reshape. One long table: 2,322 rows, 74 indicators, 8 columns.
  3. Verify. Checked every row against its source. No missing values.
  4. Analyse. Ratios, crop shares, and per-household conversions in Python and Excel.
  5. Visualise and simulate. Charts and a household simulator in this app.
Obstacles and fixes
  • Three-year averages mixed with annual data. We label every average.
  • Kwacha prices include inflation. We plot trends in international dollars.
  • No provincial data in FAOSTAT. We added Zambia Statistics Agency yields.
  • Census data is old (1990, 2000) and incomplete. We show the vintage.

Solution and tool highlights

What this app does

A data story in four questions, and a simulator that turns national averages into one household's month and one household's harvest.

Why it matters

  • Shows that hunger here is about income, soil and seed, not only total supply.
  • Makes FAOSTAT data readable without a statistics background.

Where it can be used

  • Extension officers comparing provinces and drought years.
  • Policy workshops testing who a subsidy would reach.

How it scales

  • The data model is one long table, so any FAOSTAT country can be loaded.
  • New crops and provinces add as rows, not code.

Ethical limits

  • Results are estimates from averages, not a test of any real family.
  • No personal data is collected, and every figure shows its source.
Recommendations and next steps

What we would do next

  • Feed the soil, not just the subsidyPair fertiliser support with soil testing, potassium and maize–legume rotation.
  • Measure affordability, not just harvestsTrack diet cost against income by province, and support legumes and vegetables.
  • Rebuild sorghum and millet seedExtend input support beyond maize and back community seed multiplication.
  • Close the data gapsA new agricultural census, and provincial food prices and incomes.

Future enhancements

Add sorghum and cassava to the simulator, provincial diet costs and incomes, and rainfall risk by district.

Conclusion

Zambia's hunger is not only a shortage of food. It is a shortage of income, of nutrients reaching crops, and of seed farmers can keep.

Income, soil and seed are where policy can act.
